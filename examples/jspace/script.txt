# A The silent step
"The number of legs on the animal that spins webs is..."
Eight. To get there, you thought "spider". But nobody said "spider".
A language model has to take the same silent step. This paper, from Anthropic, finds where that step happens.
# B The tower and the lens
Picture the model as a tower. The question goes in at the bottom. The answer comes out at the top.
Each layer reworks what it got and passes it up.
To look inside, the authors built a lens. For any spot in the tower, it shows which words that spot pushes the model to say.
Point it at the last word, "is", and move it up.
Near the bottom: nothing readable.
In the middle: "spider". A word that is in neither the question nor the answer.
At the top: just the answer, eight.
That middle stretch is the workspace. It holds a small set of ideas at a time, the ones the model can put into words. In the paper, it's the J-space.
# C The tests
But a word showing up doesn't prove the model uses it. So they operate.
Pull "spider" out of the middle, put "ant" in its place, and the answer at the top changes from eight to six. Ants have six legs.
Same tower, new question: think of a sport. The lens finds "soccer". Swap in "rugby", and the model says it thought of rugby.
Switch the workspace off, and simple tasks, like recalling facts, barely change. But multi-step reasoning drops to almost nothing.
# D Safety tests
The same lens works in safety tests. It shows the model weighing "leverage" and "manipulation", and reacting with "panic".
Sometimes it shows "fake" and "fictional": the model suspects it's being tested. Switch that suspicion off, and bad intentions it was hiding can come out.
# E Limits
One catch: the lens only reads ideas that fit in a single token, roughly one short word.
And the name "global workspace" comes from a theory of consciousness in neuroscience. So is this consciousness? The authors say it may or may not be.
